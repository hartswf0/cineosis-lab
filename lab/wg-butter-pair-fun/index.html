Learning a new pair of hands

I will first construct the theory of fun, then generate the experience design only after the cognitive theory is explicit.

Notation: <pattern> for what the brain recognises, [learning] for what it does, {possibility space} for the permutations it plays in. The design described in section 8 is built: open Butter Pair, tab Learn.

1. Initial interpretation

The cognitive puzzle. A person must learn a body-to-machine mapping they have never had: two cameras that each see the hand differently, one model that combines them, and a vocabulary of hand shapes that mean actions. The puzzle is not "build the tower". It is "find out what this system can see, what each shape of my hand does, and how to move so it understands me", then use that to build.

The real-world skill. Spatial control through an indirect interface: placing objects in three directions when one direction (toward and away from the laptop) is judged poorly by one camera, plus a small gesture language. It is close to learning a crane, a puppet, or sign language: comprehension through action, the premise of Total Physical Response.

What was wrong. Before this design the game told you only pass or fail. You could not tell how close you were, which control you had not learned, or whether a miss was your hand or the cameras. A brain cannot optimise against a signal it cannot read, so it had nothing to learn from.

2. Theory skeleton

Patterns

  • <pinch is grip, open is release>
  • <the band>: the region where both cameras see the hand, about 15 to 35 cm above the phone
  • <axis ownership>: the laptop camera knows across and height; the phone camera knows across and toward-and-away
  • <depth from size>: with the laptop camera alone, toward and away is read from how big the hand looks
  • <the gesture vocabulary>: two fingers up turns, two fingers flat copies, a held fist undoes, a flat palm locks a plane
  • <the brick waits>: losing the hand never drops a brick
  • <stacking is automatic>: a brick put over another sits on top

Learning actions

  • [finding the band] [calibrating the pinch] [aiming in three directions] [combining hands] [recovering] [choosing an input] (hand, touch, keys, words) [composing structures]

Boredom states

  • a drill repeated after it is mastered; a blueprint whose answer is a fixed picture, solved once; calls that come in a predictable order; a score that does not change with skill.

Constraints (frustration points)

  • tracking noise the player cannot see; the laptop camera's poor depth; misses with no explanation; permissions and setup before any play; not knowing which controls exist.

Invariants

  • a placement is either on the outline or off by a countable number of studs, rows and levels; a drill's grade comes from what the cameras measured, not from how long you tried; nothing you do loses work (undo, cancel, the brick waits).

4. Operational description

<noise> (a hand the system half-sees) [transforms into] <the band> when the first drill holds you there for 1.5 s and says which cameras see you.
<a gap between fingers> [transforms into] <pinch> when the pinch drill shows the gap as a bar with its two thresholds and counts flickers.
<a carry> [transforms into] <aim> when every carry shows, live, how many studs and rows you are off, and every placement is graded in those units.
<player> [performs] [optimisation]: moves slower, stays in the band, uses the hand that is seen best. The grade rewards exactly those habits.
<mastery> (two of three marks) [enables] the next skill and [blocks] repetition: mastered drills drop out of "next skill".
<mastered controls> [transform into] {structures}: challenges ask for a property (four high, a span with a gap, three steps rising toward the laptop, a wall eight studs long) and accept any build that has it.
<fluency> [is tested by] call and response (Builders): the calls speed up as you answer them and slow down when you miss.

5. Failure description

  • The Alexandrine solution. Touch, keys and Tell it bypass the hand entirely. That is allowed for work, but in Learn a control done by key or touch is graded one mark at most and says so ("turned with the R key, not the hand"), so mastery means the hand.
  • Blaming the player for the cameras. Every drill records how often each camera saw the hand. If either missed it more than 30% of the time over at least a second of readings, the result says so and that it is the system's part, not yours.
  • Grinding. Retrying until a lucky placement: a placement drill allows three tries and the mark drops after the first.
  • Noise frustration. A drill you cannot see progress in: every drill has a progress bar and, where it matters, a live meter (pinch gap with thresholds, studs off, which cameras read what).
  • The laptop-only open. With only the laptop camera, the first open holds instead of placing; the drill now says "pinch and open again, or press Enter".

6. Change test

What would change if the player masters this instantly? The drills would be a checklist done in two minutes, and only the open-ended parts would hold attention. So the design puts the open-ended parts after the drills:

  1. Properties instead of pictures. Challenges have many right answers and score economy (fewer bricks, higher mark). A master plays for the shortest solution or an unusual one; the best one is kept.
  2. Tempo. Builders shortens the window after each answered call (from 8 s down to 3.5 s) and lengthens it after a miss, so the difficulty follows the player.
  3. Precision as the next frontier (not yet built): once every drill has two marks, the outline tolerance could tighten, and blueprints could be generated at random so no answer is memorised.

7. Implementation plan

  • A tab row on the game panel: Learn, Blueprints, Challenge, Builders, Free.
  • Learn: eleven drills, each with a scene, an outline where needed, a judge that grades from camera readings, a progress bar, a live meter, and a result in stars plus numbers plus a fault line. Marks are saved.
  • Blueprints: each placement graded on the spot ("Exact", "2 rows too near to you"), and a report card when the build is finished: placed exactly, misses by direction, share by hand, how often both cameras saw the hand, and what to practise next.
  • Challenge: four property checks on the height map of the bed, each with live progress.
  • Events from the hand model (lift, place, turn, copy, undo, lose, find) feed every judge, so all three modes grade from the same record.

Two marks count as mastered. "Next skill" goes to the first drill without two marks. After two or three marks the next drill starts by itself after three seconds. Every result also goes into the Problems log.

Challenges

Tower: a stack four high (three marks with four bricks). Bridge: a raised brick supported at both ends with a gap under it (three marks with three bricks). Stairs: three steps, each one higher, rising toward the laptop (six bricks). Wall: two high and eight studs long (four bricks). Each shows its live measure ("tallest stack 2 of 4") and keeps your best solution.

Blueprints

Every placement is graded where you made it, in studs, rows and levels. The report card names the direction you miss in most and the drill that practises it.

9. Theory-design mapping

Where it is
Mechanicsthe drill judges read the same events the hand model produces; the pinch meter shows the real thresholds; placements are measured in the bed's own units
Rewardsmarks out of three, the numbers behind them, mastered count, best challenge solutions, a rising sound for progress and a chime for a clean result
Challengesfirst-try placement, hand-only marks, fewer bricks, faster calls
Ambiguitieschallenges accept any form that has the property; Builders mixes calls at random from what is possible on the bed

Does it survive optimisation?

The cheapest strategies are: use keys or touch (capped at one mark in Learn), stay slow and safe (rewarded, which is the habit the system needs), and memorise blueprints (the challenges have no picture to memorise). The grade cannot be raised without the hand doing the thing, so optimising the score is learning the controls. Builders adapts its tempo to the player, so it does not flatten into a treadmill until the 3.5 s floor, where the limit is the cameras' frame rate, not the player.

10. Residual human element

The design measures where a brick ends up, how fast, and who saw the hand. It does not measure whether a build is good. A tower four high made of four identical bricks and one made of a staircase of colours both pass. The choice between them, what the bridge looks like, what you build in free play, what you tell it to make, is where the game stops being a puzzle with an answer. The other part it cannot capture is the feel of the hand becoming fluent: the moment the band stops being something you look for and becomes where your hand already is. No meter shows that; the drills only lead up to it.
