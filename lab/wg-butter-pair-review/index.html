BUTTER PAIR · engineering review · 2 November 2026

Making two cameras see each other

Four rounds of changes made the two-camera system harder to use, not easier. This review says why, surveys every physical signal that could tie a laptop and a phone together (light, sound, motion, touch, the hand itself), names what the system must never do, and proposes one redesign with tests your real hardware must pass before anything else is added.

Why it got worse

  1. Everything was tuned on a simulator, none of it on your cameras. Every number in the proof pages comes from two ideal pinhole cameras and a perfect 21-point hand. Each fix made the simulator better and moved further from the room you are in.
  2. The laptop camera's position was never measured, only guessed. Corner pinches, then flashes, then hand learning, then a hand-entered model. Each is a different guess, and no two were checked against each other.
  3. The hand was used to calibrate the cameras that are supposed to measure the hand. A hand is soft, changes shape, and its size varies by person by about 15%. Using it as the ruler makes every error circular.
  4. The geometry was wrong for the work, and nothing said so. A laptop screen opened to 110° points its camera about 20° up. From 50 cm away it cannot see the table near the phone, and the phone camera cannot fit a whole hand in its picture below about 16 cm. The only place both see a hand is 20 to 40 cm above the phone. A hand held low over the Floor, where it feels natural, is seen by neither camera properly.
  5. The system never says when it is wrong. When the two cameras disagree, it averages them and draws one confident cursor. A disagreement of 5 cm looks the same on screen as agreement.
  6. Each round added a feature instead of a test. Scope, Rig, flash, touch, record: each is useful, but without a pass/fail test on real hardware, none of them can show that the system works.

The space, drawn to scale

Fields of view used: laptop 62° side to side; phone front camera 78° side to side, about 63° across its short side. The phone's camera sees a direction only if it is within about 31° of straight up across the short side, 39° along the long side. Turning the Floor so its long side points at camera A widens that.

The principle that falls out: light gives position, sound gives time and one distance, motion sensors give tilt and moved-or-still, touch gives ground truth at one height, and the hand only gives a check. The current system asks the hand to do the first job. That is the root fault.

Mission figure

  • Central figure: one hand, one position, one grasp, known to both devices.
  • Load-bearing claim: the two cameras' views can be put in one frame accurately enough (about 1 cm) that a hand seen by either is the same hand in the same place.
  • Mission objective: a person can pick up, carry and place without looking at a debug panel, and the system says plainly when it cannot.

What stays human judgment

  • Where the devices go. The table, the light and the posture are yours; the system can only say whether the result has a shared space. Owner: you.
  • What counts as "meant". Whether a gesture was intended is decided by the person; the system only counts commands you undo. Owner: you, by undoing.
  • When good enough is good enough. The tolerance (10 mm, one stud) is a design decision, not a measurement. Owner: you.

The redesign: one launch checklist, three references, one health state

Component: calibration and registration. Current behaviour: several separate rituals (Calibrate, Rig, Scope, touch, flash, hand learning). Each hides what it found. None checks the others. The working view shows a confident cursor whether or not the cameras agree.

Launch replaces Calibrate, Rig and Scope as the way in. It is a flight checklist on both screens at once, about a minute long. Each line turns green only on evidence, and the results are recorded as a baseline.
  1. Floor level and still. Motion sensors. Green under 3° and not moving.
  2. Clocks and distance. Each device beeps once; both record both. Gives the clock offset and the laptop-to-phone distance. Green when the distance agrees with the rig within 3 cm.
  3. Camera A finds the Floor. The Floor flashes five dots. If A cannot see them, the line says so and offers two routes: hold a third device showing dots in the shared space, or use the rig model, marked as measured by hand.
  4. Floor camera map. Touch three dots and lift. Green when a fourth touch, not used in the fit, lands within 10 mm.
  5. Both palms flat. Hold both hands flat, palms down, at the height the Floor shows (inside the shared band), for two seconds. Both cameras record what they see: size, palm area, landmark spread. This is the baseline. The two hands also check left and right: A and B must agree which hand is which.
  6. Gestures. Pinch, V flat, V up, fist, palm to the laptop: each once, with the camera that owns it named on screen. Green when the owning camera reads it and no other command fires.
After launch, the working view shows the bricks and one health state, not numbers. The baseline is compared all the time: if A sees your flat palm 20% smaller than at launch, A has moved, and the state says so.

How the six principles shape it

PrincipleIn this redesign
Surface the invisibleThe shared space is drawn on the Floor as the band where both cameras see you. Every estimate carries its source per axis (B for plan, A for height) and the age of the last passed check.
Overcome cognitive limitsOne health state instead of panels. The checklist is the only setup; it remembers its baseline, so the next session starts with a check, not a ritual.
Support multiple modesThree views of the same model: the bricks (work), the rig (space, to scale), the scope (evidence, on request). Same numbers, different purpose.
Direct manipulationMoving the Floor is an action: its sensors pause it, set-down re-checks it. Dragging the rig's screen angle moves the shared band live, and the band shows on the Floor.
Semantic zoomHealth state → per-camera state → per-axis source and residual → raw landmarks. Each level says the same thing in more detail.
Constrained playNothing irreversible happens in a degraded state: bricks can be lifted and carried, but placing asks for a confirming pinch when only one camera sees the hand. Undo always works.
