How it works, and what to do when it does not

Two cameras watch one hand. Each is good at two directions and poor at the third. This page explains which, gives the procedure that makes the system work, shows how to read the Plan, and ends with a troubleshooting tree that starts from what you see.

Start-up flow

Getting both devices ready happens in one fixed order. The laptop is the top row (lime), the phone the bottom row (orange). In the app, More, then Start-up flow, shows this same drawing live: the step you are on is outlined, finished steps turn green, a failed one turns red and goes into the Problems log.

Why this order

  1. The phone's permissions come first, in one tap. The camera and (on an iPhone) motion are asked for when you tap Turn on camera. A permission prompt takes the browser out of full screen, so asking for permissions after filling the screen undoes it. That is what bounced you around before.
  2. Sideways before full screen. Browsers only hold the screen in landscape (lock the orientation) once the page fills the screen. Turning the phone first means the lock holds what you already have. This step finishes on its own when the phone is landscape.
  3. Full screen gets its own tap. Browsers allow full screen only directly from a tap, and only if nothing was awaited in between. On an iPhone it is not available to web pages; the step says to use Add to Home Screen instead, and Continue moves on.
  4. Flat last, and which way round. The phone lies screen up with its long edge toward you and the top of its screen toward the laptop. When Go finds the laptop camera, it checks this: if the laptop is off to one side (the third real run found it 89° off), it asks you to turn the phone and finds the camera again. Otherwise toward and away would run along the wrong side of the bed. The motion sensors check that the phone lies flat and still. With no motion readings (motion refused, or a phone without sensors), you confirm with a button.
  5. Nothing is asked on its own afterwards. If full screen drops later (rotation, another app), a small Full screen button appears at the top; it never asks by itself. If the phone is turned upright, a notice asks to turn it back.

The laptop shows the room code and a QR code inside the Go card, not in a window over it, with the phone's five steps ticking off underneath.

What each camera sees

Under the bed, three panels show what the system is working from. Left, the setup seen from your right, as paper cut-outs: the laptop and its camera (lime), the phone and its camera (orange), each camera's view as a translucent cone, and your hand as a puppet. Each camera's guess is pinned where it thinks your hand is, with a thread back to the camera and a strip showing how unsure it is in its weak direction (the laptop camera toward and away, the phone camera in height). Middle and right, each camera's own picture with the hand it found drawn over it, and a line saying what it reads and why it lost you ("too close to the phone", "above its view").

Whenever the state changes (both see you, one loses you, neither sees you, the phone moved), the panel plays a short scene: the view pushes in toward the camera that changed, its cone dims, and a paper tag drops in saying what happened. The phone shows the same tag. More, then Film: how it sees plays a 40-second film of the same model, driven by a simulated hand through every state, with a caption for each beat (open the film). Hide the panels with Hide; bring them back from More.

On a blueprint, every placement is graded where you made it ("Exact", "2 rows too near to you"), and a finished build gets a report card: parts placed exactly, misses by direction, how much was done by hand, how often both cameras saw your hand, and which drill to practise.

Challenge asks for a property, not a picture: a stack four high, a bridge with a gap under it, three steps rising toward the laptop, a wall two high and eight studs long. Any build that has the property counts; fewer bricks score more marks. The reasoning behind all of this is on the theory of fun page.

Building

Butter Pair opens as a building game. The laptop shows the bed, the blueprint you are building and a tray of parts. The phone shows the same bed from above with the blueprint's outlines and the same tray down its right side. Everything technical (cameras, calibration, the Plan, tests, logs) is behind Workshop.

  1. Get a part. Tap it in the tray, on the laptop or on the phone. It appears on the bed, in the first free spot nearest you.
  2. Put it where the outline is. Pinch it, carry it, open over the outline; or drag it with a finger on the phone or the mouse. The level is automatic: a brick put over another sits on top of it. An outline marked L2 or L3 sits on that level.
  3. Watch the outline fill. A part in the right place, colour and level fills its outline and its square in the progress row. A part elsewhere counts as off the plan; it stays there until you move it.
  4. Finish. When every outline is filled you get up to three stars: one for building it, one for using no more lifts than parts plus one with nothing off the plan, one for no undos and under 30 seconds a part. Next blueprint, or Again.

The four blueprints are Wall, Stairs (each step one row toward the laptop: practice for the hardest direction), Bridge and Gate. Free build clears the bed for your own model. The bed is saved as you go and comes back after a reload. Export LDraw saves a .ldr file that opens in LDraw programs such as LeoCAD, Stud.io or BrickLink's viewer.

The phone fills its screen at the Full screen step of its setup (Android and iPad). An iPhone does not allow a web page to take the whole screen: use Share, then Add to Home Screen, and open Butter Pair from that icon.

Builders, and telling it

Builders is a call-and-response game. The architect calls a word or a short phrase, out loud and in large letters on both screens; you answer with your hand, and the cameras judge it. Meaning is what the move does, the way Wittgenstein's builders use "Slab!" and the way Total Physical Response teaches a language by acting it out. Get it and the next call comes sooner; miss it and the calls slow down, like a clapping game finding its tempo.

Every missed call goes into the Problems log with what each camera read at that moment, and the panel lists the calls you and the cameras agree on least. So the game is also how the system learns where it fails.

Tell it works the other way round, after Winograd's SHRDLU: type (or, where the browser has speech input, say) what to build, and it does it in the blocks world. It understands: add a blue 2x2, put the red on the blue, move it toward the laptop 3, move the green left, turn it, remove the yellow, undo, clear, and questions: where is the red, what is on the blue, how many yellow. "It" is the brick last mentioned. Everything it does can be undone.

How it works

Camera A is the laptop camera. It faces you. Camera B is the phone's front camera; the phone lies flat between you and the laptop, screen up, and is called the Floor. Each camera's tracker finds 21 points on your hand. From those, each camera estimates where the pinch is in one shared frame: across (left and right), along (toward and away from the laptop), and up (height above the phone).

DirectionCamera A (laptop)Camera B (phone, facing up)Both together
Acrossgood: position in its picturegood: position in its picturecombined
Toward and awaypoor: judged from how big your hand looksgood: position in its pictureB decides
Heightgood: position in its picturepoor: judged from how big your hand lookswhere the two lines of sight meet

Judging distance from apparent size depends on knowing how big your hand is. People's hands differ by about 15%, so before it is measured, a camera working from size alone can be several centimetres off. With both cameras on, each covers the other's poor direction, and the hand is where the two lines of sight cross: in simulation, within a few millimetres. With only the laptop camera, toward and away is the weak direction. That is why moving a brick toward or away from the laptop is the hardest move when the phone's camera is off.

The procedure

Follow the big icon on the laptop and the cue on the phone. Each step waits for evidence before it turns green; a step that cannot pass falls back to something that still works and says so.

  1. Set up. Phone flat on the table, screen up, between you and the laptop, about 40 cm from the laptop screen. Laptop screen opened normally. On the laptop press Floor, scan the code with the phone, then follow the phone: it shows one step at a time: Turn on camera (allow the camera, and motion on an iPhone), turn it sideways, Fill the screen, lay it flat. See Start-up flow.
  2. Press Go on the laptop. It checks four things in order:
    1. Both cameras on. Waits for the phone's camera. If the phone never starts, Go continues with the laptop camera, mouse and keyboard.
    2. Floor level and still. From the phone's motion sensors.
    3. Laptop camera located. The phone flashes dots; the laptop camera finds them and works out where it is. If it cannot see the phone, tilt the screen forward until it can, then back: the laptop follows the tilt from its own picture. Failing that, the Rig page, or your lifts in step 4.
    4. Both cameras solved. Touch each lit dot on the phone with a pinch, lift straight up, still pinched, and open at the top. The last dot is a check. Skipped if the phone's camera is off.
  3. Press Practice. First toward and away: if the phone's camera is off, hold your open hand still over the middle of the phone for a second (this measures your hand), then move your hand to each lit band. Then both palms flat, then seven gestures, each read by the camera that can see it.
  4. Build. Pinch over a brick to lift it, carry it, open to place it. Undo with Z or a held fist.

Toward and away from the laptop

This is the hardest direction. Here is what the system does about it, and what you can do.

What the system does

  • Whole rows, with a margin. While you carry a brick and only the laptop camera sees you, the brick moves one row at a time. It changes row only when your hand crosses one of two dashed lines in the Plan's side view. The lines sit further apart when the reading is less certain, so a wavering reading does not make the brick wander.
  • Counted from where you picked it up. The laptop camera knows roughly where your hand is but knows well how far it has moved. So rows are counted from the brick's starting row: push 2 rows' worth toward the screen and it moves 2 rows. In simulation, a laptop-only reading that was 2 to 4 rows off in absolute terms still put the brick in the right row this way.
  • Zero. Holding your open hand still over the middle of the phone tells the laptop camera how big your hand is, which is what its distance is made from. Practice does this first when the phone's camera is off.
  • An honest band. The laptop camera alone tells rows apart only about one in two. The practice band never narrows below plus or minus one row for it. With the phone camera on, it narrows to half a row.

What you can do

  1. Move the whole forearm, slowly, with your fingers held the same way. Changing your hand's shape changes how big it looks, which the laptop camera reads as distance.
  2. Keep the hand open and facing the screen when only the laptop camera sees it.
  3. Set depth exactly another way while carrying: ↑ ↓ arrow keys or the mouse wheel move one row a press; touching the phone where you want the brick places it there.
  4. Turn the phone's camera on. With both cameras, toward and away is measured by the phone and is the most accurate direction.

Recovery

These hold whatever fails:

  • A brick in the air is never dropped because a camera lost the hand. It waits; pinch where it is to go on.
  • Esc (or X on the phone) cancels a carry: the brick returns to where it was.
  • Z undoes the last change, as many times as you like.
  • More, then Recover puts held bricks back, forgets hands, stops any test or calibration, and restarts a laptop camera that stopped giving pictures.
  • Every Go step that fails falls back: no phone camera, then laptop camera with keys and touch; no flash, then tilt, then Rig, then lifts; no lifts, then the map from before.
  • If the page itself misbehaves, reload both the laptop and the phone and join again. The bed is saved and comes back.
  • The Problems log (More, then Problems log, or the red line under the status) lists every failure: what failed, the state the system was in, which cameras saw your hand, both cameras' frame rates, and a link to the branch of this tree. Each entry keeps the three seconds of hand readings before it. Save report writes one file with all of it, the last 20 seconds, the phone's camera status, the calibration and the Go record. Send that file when something goes wrong.

The behaviour described here was checked with two simulated cameras and a simulated hand. On your laptop and phone, send the launch record (Go, then Save record) and a screenshot of the phone's stage list when something fails; they say which branch of this tree you are on.
