Wink Blink Studio

The same two eyes and the same red thread, in ten situations. In every one, a blink and a wink look nearly alike. A blink happens to the mark: a machine pass, a cut, a pull on the thread. A wink is something the mark does to someone.

Each short was built the way a hand-drawn studio works. It starts as one sentence of action, then numbered key drawings, then breakdowns and an exposure sheet. The scene is split into planes at different depths, and the camera moves only when the move shows something new.

Keys onlyPlays just the key drawings, each held until the next. If the story still reads, the keys work.
Onion skinGhosts the drawings four frames either side, the way an animator flips paper to check arcs and volume.
Flat cameraPuts every plane at the same depth. What disappears is what the parallax was doing.
Beats and keysClick any beat or key drawing to jump to it. The exposure sheet lists every key's frame and how long it holds.

Twenty-four constructions

The same mark built twenty-four ways, each one blinking and then winking. They range from engraving weight to poster weight, from film perforations to an archive stamp, from a 16-pixel grid to a hand-cut woodblock. The idea should survive every one.

The four boys in short 03 come from Gilbert Ryle's example of the twitch and the wink, as retold by Clifford Geertz in “Thick Description” (1973). The wording here is a paraphrase, not a quotation. Every drawing on this page is generated live in the browser, frame by frame.
