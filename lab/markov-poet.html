How it works

One sentence, through the engine, live.

loading the archive to run the example (about 20 MB, once)
1Split the words into lines.

A line break, a comma, "while", "but", "after" start a new line. A blank line starts a new scene.

2Weigh every shot against each line.

CLIP reads the line and all 16,153 shots. Each shot gets a chance. Tap a line above to see what it weighs.

3Give each scene a world.

The engine picks the one film that can play the most of the scene's lines, and cuts inside it, forward in its time.

4Let the grammar pick the edit.

"Every evening" returns to the same place. "While" and "across the city" cut between two people. "Cannot leave" never shows the leaving. "But" holds the shot.

5Play the cut.

The coloured line under each shot is its Cineosis sign: the kind of image it is (colours below).

PLAY IT IN THE POET
6Change a shot. Beat the score. Send it.

In any room, tap CHOOSE (or a tile) to replace a shot, then SEND: a project file a friend opens and remixes, a finished MP4, or a link.

Score = 50 × relations kept + 30 × worlds held + 20 × daring. Relations kept: what the words say that the cut shows (a return, an alternation, a refusal). Worlds held: how often a cut stays inside its scene's film. Daring: the surprise of your own choices, in bits (a shot the engine gave 3% is 5 bits). Choose shots the engine did not expect, and keep the relations.

Why "Markov"

In 1913 Andrey Markov counted which letter follows which in Pushkin's Eugene Onegin. Each choice here works the same way: the next shot depends on the line and on the shots already cut. The machinery says which shots were possible. It does not say which one you kept.

So every room keeps the path: what was offered, how sure the engine was, and where somebody chose otherwise. A class is not a path.
