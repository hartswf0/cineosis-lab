The Markov Poet

A class is not a path.

In a courtyard in the Moroccan countryside, a poet builds a poem while he sings it, out of a bounded inherited stock and strict schemes of rhyme, aimed at the people close enough to hear themselves become the poem. Clifford Geertz, describing it, reaches for a comparison: the poet works molecularly, "like some artistic Markov process." The formula is inherited. The poem is still an event. Somebody whistles; the poet hears him; the next line changes.

This project makes films the same way. A finite archive of moving images, sixteen thousand shots, is the stock. Language arrives, a poem, a nursery rhyme, a meeting, a voice, and each line narrows what can follow, one choice pressing on the next. The machinery is Markov's and Shannon's: a repertoire, a state, a distribution over what comes next. The interest is in what the machinery cannot say about itself: what was offered, what was taken, what somebody saw and refused.

The path, not the class

"AI-generated" names machinery. It says nothing about what happened. These rooms keep the path instead, at every line:

Offeredthe shots in contention, and how many were really in play
Weighedthe confidence, spread across them: decisive, or open
Takenthe edit made: stay in the scene's world, cut away, return, hold
Refusedevery shot a person tried and kept instead, counted

And one test borrowed from pinball: call the shot. Before the film is made, say what must survive from the words (a thing that happens every evening, one person waiting while another leaves, someone who cannot leave) and let the film fail it. The engine reports what survived. It does not decide what mattered.

A mechanism tells us how possibilities became available. It does not tell us what somebody did with them.

Lineage

The Markov Poet grew out of the Cineosis Lab, where a periodic table of cinematic signs and Christian Metz's grand syntagmatique were built to describe how films hold together. Its engine was first cut against the fourteen poems of Where You Go When You Leave, and it is being tuned toward the work of Mark Anthony Thomas. The archive is the Moving Image Archive's public film collection. Voices are the poet's recordings and Piper. Words are read by CLIP, in the browser.
